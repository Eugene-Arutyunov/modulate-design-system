Why You Can’t Seriously Say “I’m Not a Designer”

Abstract

If you make things, you’re a designer — the only question is how good. Caring across a thousand small decisions is why the Modulate Design System is now public.

Everyone is a designer

You designed something today. Maybe it was a slide for a team meeting. A vibe-coded app. The printed sign for your garage sale.

If you made something — anything — you designed it.

And here’s the uncomfortable part: if it looks bad, you don’t get to say “well, I’m not a designer.” You are. You’re just not a good one yet.

Design is not a job title

Design is a discipline. A craft. And like any craft, it lives in a thousand small decisions: single spacing or double, this sentence or a shorter one — or an image instead. The default Google template or a custom one. These are all design decisions.

And you make them whether or not you notice. That’s the part most people miss: “I’m not a designer” isn’t opting out of design, it’s opting out of deciding. The decisions still get made — by a template, by a default, by whatever took the least time. Someone designed that slide. It just wasn’t you.

Doing good design, and spending the extra time on it, is a choice.

Some people will tell you: who cares? If it works, it’s good. Pixel-perfection is for nerds. Better design won’t make you more money tomorrow.

And here’s another uncomfortable truth: they might be right. Not tomorrow.

But I’m convinced caring pays off, from two directions. People notice who cares — and they look for them: to use their products, to work with them, to recommend them. Loyalty can’t be bought; it can only be earned. Slowly. Consistently. Over years.

And caring is a habit, not a task. The person who won’t ship a misaligned button is usually the same person who rewrites the confusing Slack message, keeps their head in a stressful situation, and tells the customer the bad news early. The standard travels.

I want to live in a world where people care about what they make and are proud of it — where “good enough” isn’t the default and craft isn’t a luxury. In the time of AI slop I feel it stronger than ever.

I’m lucky: at Modulate I found people who care the same way. Together we’re doing our best to build a useful, efficient, and ethical voice AI platform for deep conversation understanding — and the same standard runs through all of it.

That’s why we’re opening our design system

Today we’re making the Modulate Design System public. It’s live at design.modulate.ai/design-system — open it and look through it.

We’ve spent many months building it: tokens, components, patterns, principles — the whole thing. It’s how we make sure caring scales, that quality isn’t a happy accident but a system. We believe the habit of caring about small details carries into everything we build: the standard that shapes a button is the standard that shapes the model underneath it.

To be transparent about the scope: right now the system is built primarily for interfaces and vibe-coding. Paste it into your AI coding assistant and get a clean, consistent UI out of the box. Presentations, marketing materials, social media — that’s where we take it next, very soon.

We could have kept it internal. Most companies do. But if design is about caring, then sharing what we’ve learned is part of the craft.

So: look through it, take what’s useful, use it in your own work. We’re packaging it up for GitHub so you can fork it and make it properly yours — that part is coming soon, and we’ll write about it separately.

And we’re starting to write

Alongside the system, we’re opening a blog about good design and the reasoning behind our decisions — not just what we chose, but why. Posts on composition, color, icons, and why the main axis of any layout is time, not X and Y. The trade-offs, the mistakes, the details most people never see.

One thing you can do today

Look at anything you made recently — a slide, a doc, a form. Find two things that belong together and check how far apart they are compared to everything else around them. A heading and its paragraph. A label and its input. A number and its unit. If the gap inside the pair is the same as the gap outside it, the reader has to work out the grouping on their own, and some of them will get it wrong. Two identical product grids. Spacing is the only difference.
