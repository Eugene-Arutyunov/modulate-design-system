Why Have Your Own Graphic Style

Abstract

A brand is the imprint of user experience in someone’s memory, and it only forms when the experience comes with sensory input unlike anything else. Your own graphic style is that input.

What kind of relationship does a business have with graphic design?

The case for UX design is fairly clear: from the user’s perspective, the interface of a digital product is indistinguishable from the product itself. The interface experience is, ultimately, the customer experience. (In our case this applies not only to the graphical interface but to API design as well — the same logic holds.)

The value of marketing design is equally obvious — making something great isn’t enough; you have to tell people about it. Marketing design is inseparable from marketing itself (building a narrative and delivering it through channels), and that clearly affects business outcomes.

What about graphic design, then? Why invest in developing your own graphic style and visual language?

The downsides are obvious:

  • it takes expertise and effort;
  • early results may fall short of what’s freely available in one click (being less consistent, for instance);
  • and the process itself invariably sparks debate and creates organizational overhead.

The benefits only show up over the long run. Let’s trace the logic step by step.

Any business’s most valuable asset is customer trust. Trust is built through the experience of interacting with a product. Communication can also build or erode trust, since it too generates experience. To convert the short-term effects of a good user experience into a long-term business asset, companies build brands. A consistent visual style exists for exactly this purpose.

A brand is the imprint of a user experience in someone’s memory. For that imprint to form — and not blur or fade — the experience must be accompanied by sensory input unlike anything else.

Visual language is — perhaps surprisingly — a language. Communication is a function, building a product is a function, but graphic design is not a function. Different actors use graphic design for different ends:

  • interface developers use it to make relationships between elements legible;
  • marketing channel owners use it to direct audience attention;
  • brand builders use it to construct identity — which feeds into the brand, which converts everyone else’s good decisions into a long-term business asset.

With all of that on the table, we can talk about what Modulate’s visual language actually needs to do.
